\begin{planlist}
  \item \textbf{Hook.} De novo design now produces enzyme backbones faster than
        they can be optimized. Optimization needs libraries that are large,
        diverse \emph{and} target-specific; existing methods deliver at most two
        of the three.
  \item \textbf{Move 1 --- the frontier.} GRPO fine-tuning of ProteinMPNN
        against a structure-prediction reward moves the diversity/fidelity
        trade-off rather than sliding along it: on a public benchmark,
        \textbf{[3.98]}$\times$ the pass rate at matched sequence diversity.
  \item \textbf{Move 2 --- the fragments.} Splitting designs at fixed boundaries
        and recombining across them turns $\sum_i |F_i|$ synthesized parts into
        $\prod_i |F_i|$ testable variants, and the recombinants retain
        \textbf{[XX]}\,\% of the parental pass rate --- a physically buildable
        library of \textbf{[10\^{}X]} members.
  \item \textbf{Move 3 --- the demonstration.} Three rounds of closed-loop
        optimization on a de novo PETase: a pilot library on a fluorogenic
        probe, a larger library on a PET-proximal substrate, and a third
        library designed from the accumulated sequence--activity data.
  \item \textbf{Headline.} The best construct is \textbf{[250]}$\times$ faster
        than the starting design and exceeds every characterized natural enzyme
        on the same probe.
  \item \textbf{Implication.} Function-aligned search plus manufacturing-aware
        library construction plus an experimental closed loop is a general
        recipe for de novo enzyme optimization.
\end{planlist}
